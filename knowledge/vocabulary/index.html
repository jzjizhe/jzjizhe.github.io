suck

Pronunciation: /sʌk/

Part of Speech: verb, noun

Definitions

Verb

  1. To draw something into the mouth by creating a vacuum with the lips and tongue.

    • The baby sucked milk from the bottle.
    • She sucked the juice out of the orange slice.

  2. To hold something in the mouth and draw on it.

    • He sat on the porch sucking a lollipop.
    • She was nervously sucking her thumb.

  3. To draw in a particular direction by creating a vacuum or current.

    • The vacuum cleaner sucked up all the dust from the carpet.
    • The whirlpool sucked the boat down into the water.

  4. (Informal) To be very bad or unpleasant.

    • This movie really sucks.
    • It sucks that you have to work on the weekend.

  5. “Suck up to” — to behave obsequiously toward someone in order to gain advantage.

    • He’s always sucking up to the boss hoping for a promotion.

Noun

  1. An act of sucking.
    • He took a long suck on his cigarette.
    • The baby gave a few sucks and then fell asleep.

Synonyms / Antonyms

Synonyms (draw in): draw, pull, absorb, siphon, slurp

Synonyms (informal, be bad): stink, blow (informal), be terrible

Antonyms (draw in): blow, expel, push

Etymology

From Old English sūcan (“to suck”), from Proto-Germanic sūkaną, ultimately from Proto-Indo-European sewk- or seug- (“to suck”). Cognate with Latin sūgere (“to suck”), which gives us words like “suction.” The informal meaning of “to be bad/unpleasant” emerged in American English slang in the mid-20th century and became widespread by the 1970s–80s.

hieroglyphics

Pronunciation: /ˌhaɪ.ər.əˈɡlɪf.ɪks/

Part of Speech: noun (plural; also used as adjective)

Definitions

Noun

  1. A system of writing using pictorial symbols, especially that used in ancient Egypt.

    • The walls of the tomb were covered in hieroglyphics depicting scenes from the pharaoh’s life.
    • Scholars spent decades trying to decipher Egyptian hieroglyphics before the Rosetta Stone was discovered.

  2. (By extension) Any writing or symbols that are difficult or impossible to read or understand.

    • His handwriting is like hieroglyphics — I can’t make out a single word.
    • The technical manual was full of hieroglyphics that meant nothing to a layperson.

  3. (Singular form: hieroglyph / hieroglyphic) An individual pictorial symbol used in such a writing system.

    • Each hieroglyphic could represent a sound, a word, or an entire concept.

Adjective (hieroglyphic)

  1. Of, relating to, or written in hieroglyphs.
    • The hieroglyphic inscriptions on the obelisk date back to the 13th century BCE.

Synonyms / Antonyms

Synonyms: pictographs, ideograms, glyphs, symbols, characters

Antonyms: plain text, alphabetic script

Etymology

From Late Latin hieroglyphicus, from Greek hieroglyphikós (ἱερογλυφικός), composed of hierós (ἱερός, “sacred”) + glýphein (γλύφειν, “to carve, engrave”). Literally meaning “sacred carvings,” the term originally referred specifically to the formal writing system of ancient Egypt. The word entered English in the late 16th century. The informal sense of “indecipherable writing” developed naturally from the fact that Egyptian hieroglyphics remained unreadable to Europeans for centuries until Jean-François Champollion cracked the code in 1822 using the Rosetta Stone.

crap

Pronunciation: /kræp/

Part of Speech: noun, verb, adjective

Definitions

Noun

  1. Something of extremely poor quality; nonsense or rubbish.

    • That movie was absolute crap.
    • Don’t believe that — it’s a load of crap.

  2. (Vulgar) Excrement; feces.

    • The dog left crap all over the yard.

  3. Unwanted or useless items; junk.

    • There’s so much crap in this drawer I can’t find anything.
    • Clear all that crap off the table.

Verb

  1. (Vulgar) To defecate.

    • The cat crapped on the rug again.

  2. “Crap out” — to fail or break down; also, to withdraw from something out of fear.

    • My laptop crapped out right before the deadline.
    • He crapped out of the deal at the last minute.

Adjective (crappy)

  1. Of very poor quality.
    • We had crappy weather the entire vacation.
    • That’s a crappy excuse and you know it.

Synonyms / Antonyms

Synonyms (poor quality): rubbish, garbage, trash, junk, nonsense, drivel

Synonyms (excrement): poop, dung, feces

Antonyms: quality, excellence, treasure

Etymology

From Middle English crappe (“chaff, residue from rendered fat”), possibly from Old French crappe (“siftings, waste”). The scatological sense developed in the 18th century. A common folk etymology connects it to Thomas Crapper, a Victorian plumber who popularized the flush toilet, but this is coincidental — the word predates him by centuries. The sense of “nonsense, something worthless” became widespread in the 20th century.

sue

Pronunciation: /suː/

Part of Speech: verb

Definitions

Verb

  1. To institute legal proceedings against a person or organization; to bring a lawsuit.

    • She decided to sue the company for wrongful termination.
    • He threatened to sue if they didn’t return his deposit.

  2. To make a formal legal claim for something (e.g., damages, compensation).

    • The family sued for $2 million in damages after the accident.
    • They sued for breach of contract.

  3. (Archaic/Formal) To make a petition or appeal; to plead.

    • The defeated king sued for peace.
    • He sued for her hand in marriage.

Synonyms / Antonyms

Synonyms: litigate, prosecute, bring a claim, take to court, file a lawsuit

Antonyms: settle, drop charges, dismiss

Etymology

From Middle English suen (“to follow, pursue”), from Anglo-French suer / Old French sivre (“to follow”), from Vulgar Latin sequere, from Latin sequī (“to follow”). The legal sense of “to pursue a case in court” developed in the 13th–14th century, as “following” a matter through the courts. Related to words like “pursue,” “sequence,” and “suit” (as in lawsuit).
